Different liquid types use their own specific heat, so equal volumes of two different liquids don't necessarily average to the midpoint temperature. All liquids are assumed to have a density of ≈1 g/ml, so 1 ml ≈ 1 g.
Result
The physics

Heat lost by the hotter portion equals heat gained by the cooler one. For two liquids A and B combining to volume V at target temperature T:

V_A = V · c_B·(T − T_B) / [c_A·(T_A − T) − c_B·(T_B − T)], then V_B = V − V_A.

When both liquids share the same specific heat (e.g. two water-based liquids), this reduces to the simple volume-weighted average V_A = V·(T − T_B)/(T_A − T_B).

Not modelled: heat lost to the container or the air while pouring, and the ≈4% density change of water between 100 °C and 4 °C. Both are small next to typical rounding in real use.

The physics

Melting ice absorbs 334 J/g before it even starts warming as water (latent heat of fusion) — about as much energy as warming that same gram of water by 80 °C. The ice mass needed to bring m grams of liquid from T_liq down to a target T (with T above 0 °C) is:

m_ice = m·c_liq·(T_liq − T) / [c_ice·(0 − T_ice) + L_f + c_liq·T]

where c_ice ≈ 2.108 J/g°C and L_f = 334 J/g. The number of cubes is rounded up to a whole cube, so the check below shows the slightly cooler result whole cubes actually give you.

Not modelled: the ice cube's own surface melting more slowly than this instant-mixing model assumes, and container heat loss.

Result
The physics

Newton's law of cooling: the rate of heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference with the surroundings, giving exponential decay T(t) = T_env + (T₀ − T_env)·e^(−t/τ). Solved for time to reach a target:

t = τ · ln[(T₀ − T_env) / (T_target − T_env)]

τ (the time constant) bundles the item's heat capacity and how easily heat leaves its surface — it's specific to a container, fridge and airflow, which is why calibrating from one real measurement beats a formula built from guessed numbers.

When the target is below 0 °C, three stages are chained: cooling to 0 °C at τ, a plateau while it freezes (τ·(L_f/c) / (−T_env) — independent of mass, since both mass terms cancel), then cooling on as ice at a scaled time constant τ·(c_ice/c_liquid), using the same surface heat-transfer rate.

Not modelled: the door being opened, a solid or thick item (its centre lags well behind its surface — this model assumes something thin or well-stirred), and the container's own heat capacity.
